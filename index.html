🧩 If… Conditionals

A clear guide, plus unlimited exam-style practice.

The big idea

A conditional sentence has two parts: the if-clause (the condition) and the main clause (the result).

The tense you choose shows how real or possible the situation is. The less real it is, the further back the verb goes.

How real is the situation?Tense after ifName
A general factPresent simpleZero conditional
A real possibility in the futurePresent simple (or present perfect)First conditional
Imaginary, now or in the futurePast simpleSecond conditional
Imaginary, in the past (too late to change)Past perfectThird conditional
💡 The past simple and the past perfect in conditionals are not really about the past. They are “imagination tenses”: the past simple imagines now, the past perfect imagines the past.

Zero conditional · facts

If + present simple, present simple
If you heat ice, it melts.
If I don't sleep enough, I feel tired.

It describes things that are always true. You can replace if with whenever and the meaning stays the same.

First conditional · real possibility

If + present simple, will + base verb
If it rains tomorrow, we will postpone the picnic.
If you study, you will pass the exam.
⚠️ Classic mistake: ❌ If it will rain… → ✅ If it rains…
Never use will directly after if.

Where does the present perfect fit?

In the first conditional you can use the present perfect after if to talk about something that will be finished before the result:

If you have finished your homework, you can play outside.
If she has left already, we will call her.
The present perfect is not a separate conditional. It is just a first conditional where the condition is already completed.

Second conditional · imaginary now

If + past simple, would + base verb
If I had a million dollars, I would buy an island.I don't have the money.
If I were you, I would apologise.
⚠️ Two traps:
1. The verb is in the past, but it talks about the present or future. Nothing happened yesterday.
2. With I / he / she / it, the formal form is were: If I were…, If she were…. (Was is common in everyday speech, but exams expect were.)

Third conditional · imaginary past

If + past perfect, would have + past participle
If I had studied, I would have passed.But I didn't study, and I didn't pass.
If we had left earlier, we wouldn't have missed the bus.

Use it for regrets and for “what would have happened if…”. You can't change it any more.

Clues to look for in an exam

Clue in the sentenceProbably…
always, whenever, every time, a scientific factZero
tomorrow, tonight, next week, “I'm not sure yet”First
“If I were you…”, an unlikely or impossible situation nowSecond
yesterday, last week, last year, a regret about the pastThird
Two-second check: after if you should never see will, would or would have.
